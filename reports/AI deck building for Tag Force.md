# Constrain the AI, let code enforce legality

An AI can build good Tag Force 6 (TF6) and Tag Force Special (TFSP) decks, but not by asking a language model to write a decklist. The workable design is a hybrid. Recipe co-occurrence statistics plus the card database's role tags produce scored candidates. A small search or solver assembles a legal deck under the hard rules, which TF Deck Studio's `TF6Save.problems()` already checks. An optional Claude call plans roles, re-ranks a pre-filtered pool of about 100–300 cards and explains its picks, choosing cards by database ID. The rules are simple and identical for both games: main deck 40–60, extra and side at most 15 each, at most 3 copies across main and extra, capped further by the save's own ban-list cache and by owned copies. The data situation is uneven. TFSP's **841 built-in recipes** are already extracted from the ISO, but TF6 has **zero** readable recipes because its EBOOT is encrypted, so TF6 must use Yugipedia character decks, the 202 TFSP recipes that fit its pool, and user imports. The evidence argues hard against free-form generation: the only Yu-Gi-Oh!-specific fine-tuned LLM produced legal lists **37% of the time** even after training on 837 tournament decks. Most of the machinery already exists in `tfcore`: card model, role tags, similarity scoring, ban-list fitting, deck catalog, odds math, validator and save writer. The work is three new modules (a synergy graph, a deck scorer and a builder) plus one new UI view.

## Both games share one rulebook, and the validator already exists

TF6 and TFSP store decks in the same save format: 200 slots of 436 bytes at offset 0x20, differing only in plaintext size and trunk offset. Both layouts were verified in-game ([local: tfcore/games.py](../tfcore/games.py); [local: APP_PLAN.md](../APP_PLAN.md)). The local save writer enforces **main 40–60, side ≤ 15, extra ≤ 15**. It allows copies in main plus extra up to `min(3, allowed, owned)`, where `allowed` comes from the save's ban-list cache (2 bits per card row). Fusion, Synchro, Xyz and Dark Synchro frames must sit in the extra deck. The side deck does not count toward copies, matching a deck the game itself saved with 3 in main and 1 in side ([local: tfcore/tf6save.py](../tfcore/tf6save.py)). An AI builder therefore needs no new rule engine: `problems()` is the final gate, and `write_deck` already refuses anything outside the limits.

The defaults differ by era. TF6 uses an altered March 2011 OCG list with game-original changes: Pot of Greed, Heavy Storm and Goyo Guardian are forbidden, and Brionac, Trishula and Gorz are limited ([Yugipedia: March 2011 Lists (TF6)](https://yugipedia.com/wiki/March_2011_Lists_(Tag_Force_6))). TFSP uses the October 2014 OCG list ([Yugipedia: TFSP](https://yugipedia.com/wiki/Yu-Gi-Oh!_ARC-V_Tag_Force_Special)). Konami shut the PSP list-download service on 31 March 2017 ([YGOrganization](https://ygorganization.com/pspgoawayquickly/)). Ban lists therefore vary by save, which is why reading `allowed` from the open save's trunk is correct and a hard-coded list is not. The gap is building with no save open: the game's `database/limitlist.ehp` has no parser yet, so the builder would have no ban data then ([local: APP_PLAN.md](../APP_PLAN.md)).

The card pools are where the builder's search space comes from, and they hold one correction the code needs.

| | TF6 | TFSP |
|---|---|---|
| Card numbers in local DB | 5,392 (Yugipedia: 5,311) | 7,254 (Yugipedia: "over 7000") |
| Game-only cards | 352 | 396 |
| Xyz / Pendulum | **25** / 0 | 278 / 39 |
| Synchro / Dark Synchro | 160 / 7 | 207 / 7 |
| Tuners / Dark Tuners | 207 / 7 | 249 / 6 |
| Pool ends at | Photon Shockwave (mid-2011) | Secrets of Eternity (Jan 2015) |

Counts come from `tfcore/carddb.py` run on the user's cached ISOs ([local: tfcore/carddb.py](../tfcore/carddb.py)), with pool edges from Yugipedia ([TF6](https://yugipedia.com/wiki/Yu-Gi-Oh!_5D%27s_Tag_Force_6); [TFSP](https://yugipedia.com/wiki/Yu-Gi-Oh!_ARC-V_Tag_Force_Special)). **TF6 does have Xyz monsters**, including Number 39: Utopia and Gem-Knight Pearl. Yugipedia confirms it includes ZEXAL Xyz. The comments in `tfcore/ygoprodeck.py` ("2010 card pool (no Xyz)") and APP_PLAN §1b8b ("Xyz in TF6" as a missing type) are wrong. Any builder logic that drops Xyz for TF6 would cut a real, if thin, Rank 1–5 toolbox. Pendulum matters only for TFSP's 39 early cards. No source checked confirms which Master Rule TFSP's Pendulum zones follow, and nobody has tested in-game whether either game enforces an extra-deck minimum or accepts forbidden cards in the side deck.

## TFSP brings 841 recipes, while TF6 must borrow its examples

TFSP's recipe cache holds **841 recipes**: 768 character decks (192 characters × 4 strength levels), 36 structure decks, 9 starters, 7 pack decks, 6 "repro" meta rebuilds (Tellarknight, Burgesstoma, Fire Fist, Spellbook, Dragon Ruler, Shaddoll), 6 WCS 2009–2014 champion decks and 6 "serious" meta decks (Qliphort, Madolche, HERO, Nekroz, Shaddoll). 832 of them have exactly 40 main cards, and only 18 have a side deck ([local: tfcore/recipes.py](../tfcore/recipes.py); workspace `recipes.json`). That is an in-distribution corpus with a built-in weak strength label, since the Lv1 to Lv4 ladder within each character orders decks by intended power. The project already marks 210 of them as "champion" decks.

TF6 has no equivalent. Its ISO yields 0 recipes because the EBOOT is encrypted ([local: APP_PLAN.md](../APP_PLAN.md)). Its examples have to come from three places. Yugipedia's 126 TF6 character pages carry `{{Decklist}}` templates with a 1–10 star rating, which `wikidecks.py` already parses, though the download (`<home>/wiki/TF6_pages.json`) has not been run in this workspace ([Yugipedia category](https://yugipedia.com/wiki/Category:Yu-Gi-Oh!_5D%27s_Tag_Force_6_characters)). The project estimates about 476 decks, of which roughly 150 use anime-only cards TF6 lacks. Second, 202 of TFSP's 841 main decks fit TF6's pool unchanged, including the 2009–2011 WCS champions. Third, the user's YDK imports: a project spot check found 14 of 15 sampled YGOPRODeck Edison decks fit both games completely ([Edison banlist](https://edisonformat.com/banlist.html); [YGOPRODeck formats](https://ygoprodeck.com/formats/)). YGOPRODeck's API exposes card endpoints only and no deck endpoint ([API guide](https://ygoprodeck.com/api-guide/)), so bulk scraping is off the table and the corpus stays in the hundreds, not thousands. Yugipedia text is CC BY-SA 4.0, so any shipped derived data needs attribution.

Two caveats limit what the corpus can teach. Tag Force CPU decks historically kept their original recipes regardless of ban-list updates ([Yugipedia: Tag Force 2](https://yugipedia.com/wiki/Tag_Force_2)), so recipes can break the save's current list. They must go through `similar.align/suggest/fill` before use as templates. And character decks are flavour-driven: a Lv1 deck shows what a theme looks like, not what wins. The Lv4, WCS and meta decks should carry more weight when the builder learns ratios.

## Free-form LLM decks fail; grounded hybrids succeed

No working, published Yu-Gi-Oh! deck *builder* exists. The strongest open YGO AI, ygo-agent, plays on ygopro-core and still lists "Implement AI deck building" as an unfinished roadmap item. Its strong policy took about 100M games, which the author equates to 32 RTX 4090s for 5 days ([GitHub ygo-agent](https://github.com/sbl1996/ygo-agent)). WindBot needs a hand-written C# executor per deck, so it cannot fairly score arbitrary generated decks ([IceYGO/windbot](https://github.com/IceYGO/windbot)). The one YGO-specific LLM, a LoRA-tuned Qwen3-4B trained on 837 top-cut lists, scored **0.372 legality and 0.393 spec adherence** in its best version ([featherless.ai](https://featherless.ai/models/johnjmartin97/qwen3-4b-ygo-meta-v3)). Practitioner reports show the same failures in general models. ChatGPT decks came in under 40 cards or put Extra Deck monsters in the main deck ([YGOPRODeck](https://ygoprodeck.com/deck/chatgpt-c-deck-323766)). A Famitsu test found misdescribed effects and too few starters, with hallucination worst on obscure cards ([Famitsu 2025-05](https://www.famitsu.com/article/202505/41577)). Tag Force makes this worse: its 2006–2014 pool is old, its text is Chinese in the user's patched ISO, and hundreds of cards are game-only.

Grounding fixes most of this. When a practitioner fed Claude real card data and precomputed counts instead of names, invented abilities and miscounts largely went away ([xopla.net](https://xopla.net/posts/a-little-tool-to-make-ai-better-at-magic-the-gathering-deckbuilding)). In MTG Commander, EDH-LLM built a candidate pool from embedding and feature similarity, then let ChatGPT pick from it. That beat embedding-only pools but fell short of decks built from historical play data, and the LLM over-picked generic staples at the cost of synergy ([EDH-LLM](https://evelynyee.github.io/edh-llm)). Konami's own Master Duel auto-builder fills main and extra decks from seed cards using worldwide player deck data, with no LLM ([Famitsu 2024-11](https://www.famitsu.com/article/202411/23591)). The pattern is clear: real deck data supplies the strength, and code supplies the legality. The LLM is at most a planner and re-ranker. Even its text handling needs care, because full card text lowered GPT-4o's draft accuracy from 43% to 38% compared with names alone ([UrzaGPT](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.08382v1)). The model should see a lean, pre-filtered pool, not the whole database.

Simulation-driven search, the gold standard in Hearthstone, does not transfer. DSA-ME needed 200 games per deck and 10–12 hours per trial on a 200-CPU cluster ([arXiv 2112.03534](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2112.03534)), and a single GA run "took hours or days" ([Q-DeckRec](https://arxiv.org/abs/1806.09771)). Yu-Gi-Oh! results depend heavily on the play policy, so a generic bot's win rate would mostly measure the bot. No simulator runs Tag Force's card set and game-only cards anyway. The affordable proxy is consistency math. A 40-card deck with 13 starters opens at least one about **87.7%** of the time, against 63.9% with 7 ([Cardmarket Insight](https://cardmarket.com/en/Insight/Articles/The-Mathematics-Behind-Building-Competitive-Decks)). `doctor.py` already computes these hypergeometric odds exactly. It assumes a 5-card opening hand with no first-turn draw. YGOPRODeck shows how the same math changes when going second ([YGOPRODeck](https://ygoprodeck.com/yu-gi-oh-hypergeometric-probability-distribution-function)).

## A five-stage pipeline built mostly from existing tfcore parts

The recommended builder takes a seed (one or more cards, an archetype word, or an existing deck to complete or upgrade), a game and an optional open save. It runs five stages, and each stage maps onto code that already exists or a small new module.

| Stage | What it does | Existing code | New code |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Corpus | Gather example decks for the game, ban-fit them | `deckcatalog.entries()`, `similar.align/fill`, `wikidecks`, `recipes` | none |
| 2. Synergy | Card-pair co-occurrence / PMI over the corpus; archetype groups | `tags.tags_of`, name-word archetype guess in `similar` | `tfcore/synergy.py` |
| 3. Candidates | Rank cards for the seed: PMI + text/tag similarity + staples, with content fallback for unseen cards | `similar.similar`, `tags_staples.json`, `CardSource.cards` | ranking function in `synergy.py` |
| 4. Assemble | Fill role quotas and slots under hard caps; maximize synergy + consistency | `trunk()` caps, `doctor.odds` | `tfcore/autobuild.py` (greedy/beam, or a small ILP) |
| 5. Validate | Legality gate and save path | `TF6Save.problems()`, `Library.save`, `write_deck` | none |

Stage 2 is the core new idea, and it suits small data. Co-occurrence embeddings built from player decks already power a YGO card recommender ([fferegrino listing](https://awesome.ecosyste.ms/projects/github.com%2Ffferegrino%2Fyu-gi-oh-card-vectors)). The research did not find how few decks such methods need, though. For TFSP, 841 recipes give dense enough pairs within archetypes. For TF6, a few hundred wiki decks plus the 202 compatible TFSP recipes will be thin, so the content side must carry more weight there. Card features and text are exactly what let MTG models predict about 55% of human picks on cards never seen in training ([arXiv 2407.05879](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.05879)). `similar.py`'s Chinese bigram-Jaccard plus 13 role tags already gives that content signal, scoring all 5–7k cards in about 7–40 ms ([local: tfcore/similar.py](../tfcore/similar.py)). Recipe weights should follow strength, with Lv4, WCS and meta decks above Lv1, so the graph learns how good decks look rather than flavour decks.

Stage 4 should be deterministic. Role quotas come from the corpus: average monster/spell/trap ratios, Tuner counts and searcher counts per archetype or level tier. No external source gives era-appropriate ratios, and the user's recipes are the best evidence for this era's trap-heavy style. The objective adds pairwise synergy to a consistency term (P(open ≥ 1 starter) from `doctor`). The structural checks `doctor` already flags, such as Synchros with no Tuners, Xyz ranks with no matching main-deck levels, or Dark Synchros with no Dark Tuner, become hard or soft constraints. A greedy or beam search is enough at this scale. LOCM work shows a GA tuning attribute-based card scores works well for arena deck building ([Yang et al. 2021](https://gpbib.cs.ucl.ac.uk/gp-html/Yang_2021_SSCI.html)). An ILP via OR-Tools or PuLP would make the caps exact, at the cost of a new dependency; that latency is estimated, not benchmarked. Because `align/suggest/fill` already handles copy caps and replacements, the first version can simply build an over-full ranked list and pass it through `fill`.

The LLM layer is optional and goes on top, not at the centre. Claude takes the seed, the 1–3 most similar recipes, and about 200 candidate records (ID, Chinese name, frame, level, ATK/DEF, tags, short text). It returns a role plan and a strict JSON list of `{card_id, count, reason}`. Claude's structured outputs guarantee the JSON parses but cannot express deck-size or numeric bounds: `minimum`/`maximum` and `minItems` above 1 are unsupported, and no maximum enum size is documented ([Anthropic structured outputs](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/structured-outputs)). IDs and counts are therefore checked in code. `problems()` errors go back for at most 2–3 repair rounds before `fill` fixes whatever remains. Asking explicitly for starter, extender and removal counts counters the "too few starters" and "staples over synergy" failures. Running the rules, ban list, recipe examples and tool definitions as a cached prefix keeps cost low: cache reads are 0.05× base input on Sonnet 5.5 and Opus 5.5 ([prompt caching](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-caching)). At $2/$10 per MTok for Sonnet 5.5 ([models overview](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/models/overview)), a 30K-token build with a few repair turns comes to roughly **$0.10–0.20 per deck** by the research notes' own arithmetic, which is not measured. A one-time Haiku 5.5 batch pass at the 50% batch discount could add LLM-derived tags (starter, extender, searcher target, archetype reference) to every card's Chinese text. That would strengthen the regex tags `tags.py` produces now. Small local 7–8B models were unreliable zero-shot at card selection ([UrzaGPT](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.08382v1)), so a local model only fits a narrow re-ranking role.

## Adding it to TF Deck Studio without breaking the save path

The integration fits the app's existing shape: a NiceGUI app (`app/main.py`, port 8080) over the `tfcore` library, with pytest coverage. Build in this order.

First, fix the TF6 Xyz comments, and land the planned `tfcore/adapt.py` archetype support using ygocdb setcodes. That replaces guessing archetypes from name words, which `synergy.py` will lean on. Next, add `tfcore/synergy.py`. It builds a weighted PMI table per game from `deckcatalog.entries()` and caches it next to `recipes.json` under `%LOCALAPPDATA%/TFDeckStudio`. TF6 needs the Yugipedia download run first, and a TFSP Yugipedia source would widen that corpus too. Then add `tfcore/autobuild.py` with `build(seed, game, save=None, size=40, style=None) -> (main, extra, side, report)`. The report holds per-card reasons and the `doctor` odds. The function always ends by calling `problems()`, and when no save is open it uses the Yugipedia default list or "ban list off" mode. The UI adds an "AI build" view: seed picker, game, optional style (ratio template from a chosen recipe), result grid with reasons, and swap suggestions from `similar`. The output goes to `Library.save` or into the normal save-review flow, never straight to the device. The Claude layer comes last, behind a setting with the user's API key, as a "Plan with Claude" button that calls the same `autobuild` with LLM-ranked candidates.

Evaluation can be offline and cheap. Hide 30–50% of a held-out TFSP recipe, let the builder complete it, and measure recall of the hidden cards plus how often the result passes `problems()`. That should be 100% by construction. Run it separately on Lv4/WCS/meta decks and on Lv1 decks. No cited YGO paper validates this protocol; it is standard recommender practice. New tests belong beside the existing 18 test files (about 95 tests). Fixed-seed builds on the fixture decks (battery, dragunity, junk, karakuri, morphtronic) would catch regressions in legality and role balance.

## Conclusion

Tag Force is a better target for AI deck building than modern Yu-Gi-Oh!, not a worse one. The pool is frozen, the rules fit in six numbers, the ban list sits in the save, and TFSP ships hundreds of labelled recipes. That turns the problem from open-ended generation into ranked completion inside a closed set, the regime where co-occurrence plus content features is known to work. The real asymmetry is between the two games, not between AI methods. TFSP has enough recipes to learn from directly. TF6's quality depends on pulling in the Yugipedia decks and the TFSP crossovers, and on leaning more on text-and-tag similarity.

The most valuable step is therefore not the LLM. It is `synergy.py`, the deck-level score and leave-k-out evaluation, because they make every later layer, Claude included, measurable. Once a recall number exists, adding Claude becomes a testable question: does LLM re-ranking raise recall or role balance enough to justify cents per deck and an API key? The literature suggests a modest gain on synergy and explanations, and no gain on legality, which code already guarantees.
